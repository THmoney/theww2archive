Silent Service: The U.S. Submarine Campaign Against Japan

While iconic aircraft carrier engagements like Midway and Leyte Gulf dominate Pacific War history, a silent, relentless war of attrition was waged beneath the ocean surface. Operating thousands of miles from home ports in Pearl Harbor, Fremantle, and Brisbane, the U.S. Navy Submarine Force executed a devastating blockade that strangled the island nation of Japan.

Targeting the Merchant Fleet (Maru)

As an island nation lacking native natural resources, the Empire of Japan depended entirely on a massive fleet of merchant freighters and oil tankers (*Maru*) to transport rubber, oil, iron ore, and food from its conquered territories in Southeast Asia back to the home islands.

Unlike early surface fleet strategies, U.S. fleet submarines (primarily Gato, Balao, and Tench-class vessels) carried out unrestricted submarine warfare. Systematically patrolling Japanese shipping lanes, American submariners focused on sinking oil tankers and cargo vessels rather than heavily armed warships, crippling the Japanese industrial war machine from within.

Overcoming the Mark 14 Torpedo Scandal

The initial years of the submarine campaign were plagued by major technical failures involving the standard U.S. **Mark 14 torpedo**. Submariners repeatedly reported that torpedoes ran deeper than set, exploded prematurely due to faulty magnetic detonators, or struck targets directly without exploding due to flawed contact pins.

For nearly two years, the Bureau of Ordnance dismissed complaints from submarine commanders. Only after independent testing enforced by Admiral Charles Lockwood confirmed these flaws were the firing mechanisms redesigned. By late 1943, equipped with reliable torpedoes and improved SJ surface-search radar, American submarines began inflicting catastrophic losses on Japanese shipping.

Crushing Economic Impact and Victory

By 1945, the U.S. Submarine Force—representing less than two percent of total U.S. Navy personnel—had achieved absolute naval dominance. American submarines sank over 50 percent of all Japanese merchant vessels (over 1,300 ships) and nearly 30 percent of all Japanese warships, including eight aircraft carriers and one battleship.

By cutting off aviation fuel, crude oil, and raw materials, the Silent Service paralyzed Japanese industrial manufacturing, grounded aircraft for lack of fuel, and left the home islands starving long before Allied armies reached Japanese shores.
